THE 250-YEAR ACCOUNTABILITY DRIFT | 2
Abstract
The United States was founded around two competing dangers: government can possess too 
much unconstrained power, but government can also possess too little authority to fulfill the 
responsibilities assigned to it. The Articles of Confederation demonstrated the second problem. 
The Constitution responded by creating a substantially stronger federal government while 
simultaneously dividing political authority among institutions, establishing elections, 
preserving federalism, providing impeachment mechanisms, and constructing other restraints 
intended to prevent governmental power from escaping control.
Over the following 239 years, the scale and complexity of American government changed 
dramatically. Congress enacted increasingly complex statutory regimes. Executive institutions 
expanded. Administrative agencies acquired substantial delegated responsibilities. Government 
spending and contracting grew. National-security institutions developed. Regulatory systems 
multiplied. Oversight institutions were repeatedly created to supervise institutions created by 
previous generations.
This paper asks whether the mechanisms connecting governmental authority to responsibility 
and consequence expanded at the same pace. It introduces Accountability Drift as an analytical 
construct describing the gradual separation of governmental authority from clearly identifiable 
responsibility, effective public control, and meaningful corrective consequence. Four variables 
are examined: Authority, Responsibility, Consequence, and Citizen Distance.
The evidence does not support a simple narrative of continuous governmental decline. 
American history contains powerful examples of accountability functioning successfully. The 
historical record nevertheless reveals a recurring vulnerability: governmental systems can 
become increasingly capable of detecting and documenting failure without becoming equally 
capable of producing timely correction. The paper therefore distinguishes answerability from 
accountability. The central question is not whether government can discover its failures. It is 
what happens after government knows.
Keywords: accountability; constitutional government; administrative state; oversight; 
institutional failure; political corruption; federal government; governance; consequence failure;
citizen distance
Executive Findings
- The founding era is a counterexample to the claim that less governmental authority 
necessarily means greater accountability: under the Articles of Confederation, national 
responsibility often exceeded national capability.
- The Constitution increased federal authority while simultaneously creating mechanisms 
intended to constrain that authority.
- American history contains repeated high-consequence accountability events, including 
institutional replacement, electoral transfer, civil-service reform, criminal prosecution, 
presidential resignation, and creation of new oversight institutions.

1. Introduction
In 1776, the American colonies justified independence using a proposition that became 
foundational to American political accountability: legitimate governmental authority derives 
from the governed. The Declaration of Independence states that governments derive their just 
powers from the "consent of the governed" and recognizes the people's authority to alter or 
abolish governments destructive of their purposes.
That principle established a basic accountability relationship: government does not originate 
political authority; it receives delegated authority. But declaring popular sovereignty and 
constructing institutions capable of preserving it are different problems.
The first American national system under the Articles of Confederation demonstrated this 
immediately. The central government possessed important national responsibilities while 
lacking several powers necessary to fulfill them. The Constitution responded by increasing 
federal authority. That fact prevents this investigation from beginning with the ideological 
assumption that less governmental power necessarily produces greater accountability.
The constitutional challenge is more difficult: government must possess enough authority to 
fulfill legitimate responsibilities while remaining subject to institutions and citizens capable of 
imposing meaningful consequences when authority is abused or repeatedly fails.
Source: Declaration of Independence - National Archives
2. Defining Accountability Drift
This study introduces Accountability Drift as an analytical construct rather than an established 
legal doctrine.
The gradual separation of governmental authority from clearly identifiable 
responsibility, effective public control, and meaningful corrective 
consequence.
Accountability Drift does not require criminal corruption, conspiracy, or an assumption that 
governmental expansion is illegitimate. It may emerge incrementally from individually 
defensible institutional changes. A legislature may delegate authority because technical 
expertise is required. An agency may divide responsibility because a problem crosses

3. Four Variables of Accountable Government
Authority describes what an institution can actually do, including legislative power, executive 
discretion, regulatory authority, taxation, spending, enforcement, emergency authority, 
administrative interpretation, and delegated decision-making.
Responsibility concerns whether identifiable institutions or individuals can reasonably be 
connected to governmental decisions and outcomes. Responsibility becomes harder to establish 
when authority is divided among Congress, executive departments, independent agencies, state 
governments, contractors, courts, and successive administrations.
Consequence concerns what happens after abuse or preventable failure is established. 
Consequences can include electoral defeat, dismissal, removal from authority, prosecution, 
financial recovery, judicial restraint, restructuring, termination of a program, modification of 
authority, or verified institutional reform. Consequences need not be punitive; the objective is 
correction.
Citizen Distance describes the number and complexity of institutional layers separating citizens 
from the actual exercise of governmental authority. Distance is not itself evidence of 
illegitimacy. Modern government frequently requires specialized administration. But the longer
accountability chain raises a critical question: when something fails, can citizens still identify 
where consequential authority resides?
4. Answerability, Accountability, and Consequence Failure
Governmental institutions can produce enormous amounts of oversight activity: hearings, 
reports, audits, testimony, investigations, disclosures, and recommendations. These are 
important forms of answerability.
Explanation without consequence is answerability. Accountability requires 
the realistic possibility of correction or constraint.
This paper defines Consequence Failure as a condition in which a governmental failure is 
identifiable, attributable, preventable or correctable, and supported by credible evidence, yet 
insufficient corrective action follows to prevent substantially the same failure from 
meaningfully recurring.
This definition deliberately separates consequence failure from corruption. A program can 
experience consequence failure without a single official accepting a bribe. Likewise, a criminal 
prosecution may punish an individual while leaving the institutional vulnerability that enabled 
misconduct substantially unchanged.

5. Method: The American Accountability Ledger
Historical cases are evaluated through a common analytical sequence:
Authority -> Decision -> Outcome -> Detection -> Investigation -> 
Responsibility -> Consequence -> Correction -> Verification -> Recurrence
The method changes the historical question. The important event is not merely the scandal, 
audit, hearing, or investigation. The important question is what happened after credible 
evidence existed. The same evidentiary standard is applied regardless of political party, 
administration, institution, ideology, or historical period.
Allegations are not treated as established facts. Policy disagreement is not automatically 
classified as governmental failure. Improper payments are not automatically classified as fraud.
Legality is not automatically classified as effective accountability. The historical record 
determines the classification.
6. 1776-1789: Responsibility Without Sufficient Authority
The Articles of Confederation produce the first important result in this study - and it cuts against
a simplistic theory of governmental expansion. The Confederation Congress possessed national 
responsibilities but depended heavily on states for revenue and lacked important mechanisms 
later given to the federal government for taxation, commerce, and direct execution.
The imbalance can be represented as Responsibility > Authority. Citizens could expect national 
outcomes from an institution that lacked sufficient capability to reliably produce them, while 
responsibility for failure remained distributed between Congress and the states.
The eventual consequence was extraordinary: the institutional framework itself was replaced. 
The Constitutional Convention produced a stronger federal structure, and the Constitution was 
submitted to state ratifying conventions.
Increasing governmental authority can strengthen accountability when 
additional authority corrects a mismatch between responsibility and 
capability and remains accompanied by meaningful constraints.

7. 1789-1801: The First Stress Test
The Constitution solved part of the Confederation's weakness by creating a federal government 
capable of acting more directly and effectively. That success immediately created the opposite 
danger: a government capable of enforcing law is also capable of enforcing laws that substantial
portions of the public regard as abusive.

The Whiskey Rebellion demonstrated the new government's enforcement capacity. Resistance 
to the federal excise tax on distilled spirits escalated into intimidation and violence against 
federal officials. President George Washington ultimately called militia forces into federal 
service; organized resistance collapsed without a major military battle.
The Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798 provided a different test. Enacted during conflict with 
France and intense domestic partisan competition, the laws expanded federal authority over 
aliens and criminalized certain false, scandalous, and malicious statements against the federal 
government. The Sedition Act was used against political critics.
The backlash contributed to the political environment surrounding the election of 1800. The 
Federalists lost national power, and the transition to Thomas Jefferson marked the first peaceful
transfer of presidential power between rival political parties.
This case does not establish that elections automatically correct governmental abuse. It 
demonstrates that, at this early stage, electoral consequence could produce substantial political 
change.
Source: Alien and Sedition Acts - National Archives
Source: Federalist Papers 51-60 - Library of Congress
8. Patronage and Civil-Service Reform
During the nineteenth century, political victory increasingly carried control over government 
employment. The spoils system linked public jobs to partisan loyalty and political organization. 
As federal employment expanded, concerns grew about patronage, assessments on government 
employees, competence, and corruption.
The Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883 established a merit-based federal civil-service 
system for covered positions, competitive examinations, restrictions on political assessments, 
and the Civil Service Commission. Initial coverage was limited but expanded over time.
The reform solved a real accountability problem while creating a new governance balance. 
Professional permanence increased expertise and continuity and reduced direct partisan 
patronage, but it also made the relationship between electoral change and administrative 
control more complex.
This is an early example of the Reform Paradox: correcting one accountability vulnerability can 
alter the structure in ways that create a different accountability problem to manage.
Source: Pendleton Act - National Archives
9. Gilded Age Corruption and Structural Reform
The post-Civil War period provides documented examples of public authority intersecting with 
concentrated private financial interests. In the Credit Mobilier scandal, favorable shares and 
financial relationships involving influential public officials became the subject of congressional 
investigation; Representatives Oakes Ames and James Brooks were censured in 1873.

Concerns also surrounded the original system of selecting U.S. senators through state 
legislatures. Legislative deadlocks and allegations of bribery contributed to a decades-long 
movement for direct election. The Seventeenth Amendment, ratified in 1913, transferred 
selection of senators to voters.
Direct election shortened one form of citizen distance while changing the original constitutional
relationship between state governments and the Senate. The case therefore illustrates why 
reforms must be evaluated for both the vulnerability they correct and the institutional 
relationship they alter.
Source: Credit Mobilier Scandal - U.S. House History
Source: Seventeenth Amendment - U.S. Senate
10. Administrative Capacity and Citizen Distance
Industrialization produced problems that individual states struggled to regulate effectively. The 
Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 created the Interstate Commerce Commission and placed 
interstate railroads under federal economic regulation.
The administrative model increased governmental capability by allowing specialized 
institutions to supervise complex industries continuously. It also introduced an additional layer 
between voters and many consequential governmental decisions.
Citizen -> Legislature -> Statutory Delegation -> Administrative Institution -> 
Regulatory Decision
This structure is not inherently illegitimate. But every additional layer creates another 
responsibility question: when a regulatory failure occurs, should citizens hold Congress, the 
president, agency leadership, career administrators, courts, or regulated entities responsible? 
Administrative capability can increase while Responsibility Clarity declines.
Source: Interstate Commerce Act - National Archives
11. Teapot Dome: A High-Consequence Case
The Teapot Dome scandal provides a comparatively complete accountability chain. Interior 
Secretary Albert Fall secretly leased federal petroleum reserves to private interests. Senate 
investigation exposed financial relationships surrounding the leases, and Fall ultimately became
the first former cabinet officer imprisoned for crimes committed while serving in office.
The investigation also contributed to development of congressional investigative authority. In 
McGrain v. Daugherty (1927), the Supreme Court recognized Congress's power to compel 
testimony in aid of legitimate legislative investigation.
Authority -> Secret Discretion -> Private Benefit -> Investigation -> Evidence 
-> Criminal Consequence -> Institutional Strengthening

The analytical question is why similarly complete chains appear in some circumstances but not

12. Delegation and the Modern Administrative State
Twentieth-century government increasingly relied on agencies to administer complex statutory 
programs. Congress frequently establishes policy and delegates substantial implementation 
authority to executive or independent agencies.
Delegation produces real capability. Specialists can make technical decisions, programs can 
operate continuously, and regulatory systems can adapt without Congress enacting a new 
statute for every implementation detail. But delegation also distributes responsibility.
Legislators can point to agency implementation. Agencies can point to statutory mandates. 
Executive officials can point to legal constraints. Courts can constrain or expand interpretations.
This does not itself establish Accountability Drift, but it creates structural conditions in which 
Responsibility Clarity becomes an empirical variable rather than an assumption.
Source: Congressional Research Service - Nondelegation
13. Watergate: Accountability Across Institutions
Watergate provides another high-consequence benchmark. Following the 1972 break-in at 
Democratic National Committee headquarters, journalism, criminal investigation, congressional
inquiry, judicial proceedings, and the impeachment process interacted across multiple 
institutions.
The Senate created the Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities in 1973. When 
President Richard Nixon resisted production of White House recordings, the dispute reached the
Supreme Court. In United States v. Nixon, the Court unanimously required production of 
subpoenaed recordings for the criminal proceeding. The House Judiciary Committee 
subsequently approved articles of impeachment, and Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974, before a
full House vote.
The post-Watergate period produced major reforms involving campaign finance, ethics, 
financial disclosure, transparency, and inspectors general.
Large governmental institutions can still generate powerful accountability 
when independent institutions possess sufficient authority, information, and 
willingness to impose consequence.
Source: Watergate Committee - U.S. Senate

14. Intelligence Oversight and the Problem of Secrecy
National-security secrecy creates an especially difficult accountability problem because 
legitimate secrecy can simultaneously weaken external supervision. The Church Committee 
investigations of 1975-1976 documented intelligence and domestic surveillance abuses involving
federal institutions and exposed weaknesses in prior congressional oversight.
The consequence included permanent intelligence oversight structures, including the Senate 
Select Committee on Intelligence. Once again the Reform Paradox appears: new oversight 
becomes another institution whose future effectiveness must itself be evaluated.
Source: Church Committee - U.S. Senate
15. The Modern Oversight State
By the late twentieth century, the United States had constructed not only a large administrative 
government but also a substantial oversight system around it. The Inspector General Act of 1978
established independent audit and investigative functions across major federal institutions. The 
Ethics in Government Act established broader financial-disclosure and ethics mechanisms.
These reforms are genuine evidence against a theory of uninterrupted accountability decline. 
Modern government possesses detection and oversight mechanisms unavailable to earlier 
generations.
Their existence, however, creates a new empirical question: does increased detection produce 
increased correction?
Source: Inspectors General - Oversight.gov
Source: Office of Government Ethics - History
16. Emergency Government and COVID-19
Emergencies expose the tension between speed and accountability. During the COVID-19 
pandemic, Congress authorized extraordinary relief programs intended to stabilize households, 
businesses, health systems, governments, and the wider economy. Rapid distribution was an 
explicit priority.
GAO subsequently documented extensive fraud risk, improper payments, control weaknesses, 
and program-integrity challenges. At the same time, oversight and enforcement produced 
hundreds of recommendations, significant financial recoveries and benefits, and thousands of 
criminal charges and convictions related to pandemic fraud.
The pandemic therefore demonstrates both sides of the thesis simultaneously: extraordinary 
governmental capability, extraordinary accountability risk, substantial subsequent 
enforcement, and persistent institutional weaknesses.
Source: GAO - Emergency Relief Funds
Source: GAO - Federal Response to COVID-19

17. The Modern Recurrence Problem
The strongest contemporary evidence for examining Consequence Failure may involve 
recurrence rather than a dramatic scandal. GAO reported approximately $186 billion in 
estimated improper payments for fiscal year 2025 across 64 programs reported by 15 agencies. 
Cumulative reported improper-payment estimates since fiscal year 2003 total approximately $3 
trillion.
Improper payments are not synonymous with fraud. They include overpayments, 
underpayments, payments lacking sufficient documentation, payments to ineligible recipients, 
and other errors. That distinction makes the evidence useful for this analysis: the question is 
recurring documented governmental failure, not an unsupported assumption of criminal 
corruption.
GAO also identified programs with estimated improper-payment rates of at least 10 percent 
across multiple consecutive fiscal years and continuing material weaknesses affecting the 
government's ability to determine the full extent of improper payments and assure appropriate 
corrective action.
What constitutes accountability when government detects a problem, 
measures it, reports it, legislates around it, audits it, recommends corrective 
action - and substantial forms of the problem continue?

18. The Detection-Correction Gap
The historical record suggests that the modern accountability problem may be less about 
inability to discover failure than inability to consistently convert discovery into durable 
correction. This paper calls that difference the Detection-Correction Gap.
Failure -> Detection -> Report -> Recommendation -> Acknowledgment -> ?
If correction occurs and recurrence falls, accountability has functioned. If reports accumulate 
while substantially similar failures continue, answerability may have increased without 
consequence increasing proportionally.
This proposition is falsifiable. Recommendation implementation, corrective-action timing, 
recurrence rates, responsibility clarity, and independently verified outcomes can all be 
measured.

19. What 250 Years Show
The evidence does not support a simple conclusion that American government experienced 250 
uninterrupted years of declining accountability. At several major moments accountability 
worked powerfully: the Confederation structure was replaced; controversial federal action 
encountered electoral consequence; patronage generated civil-service reform; Teapot Dome 
produced prosecution and stronger investigative precedent; Watergate produced presidential 
resignation and institutional reform; intelligence abuses produced new oversight structures.
At the same time, modern accountability has become increasingly institutionalized. Citizens 
often depend upon auditors to detect, inspectors general to investigate, agencies to respond, 
Congress to oversee, courts to review, prosecutors to enforce, ethics offices to disclose, and 
subsequent administrators to implement corrections.
Modern accountability is therefore simultaneously more capable and more indirect. That is the 
paradox at the center of Accountability Drift.
20. A Provisional Accountability Drift Model
Future empirical work can test Accountability Drift using Authority Volume, Responsibility 
Clarity, Citizen Distance, Transparency, Oversight Independence, Consequence Velocity, 
Correction Strength, Verification, and Recurrence.
The hypothesis would gain support where governmental authority, complexity, permanence, or 
Citizen Distance increase while Responsibility Clarity, Consequence Velocity, Correction 
Strength, or verification fail to keep pace. Evidence showing that accountability capacity grew 
proportionally or faster would weigh against the hypothesis.
21. Limitations
Comparing institutions across 250 years is inherently difficult. Government responsibilities, 
population, technology, economic scale, communications, administrative capability, and 
democratic participation changed dramatically.
Greater detection of modern governmental failure does not necessarily mean modern 
government fails more frequently; improved auditing may make failures more visible. 
Historical records are also uneven, and assigning responsibility can be difficult when outcomes 
result from multiple institutions.
Unsuccessful policy should not automatically be classified as accountability failure. Democratic 
government must be capable of experimentation and legitimate disagreement. Accountability 
can also involve competing values: protecting civil servants from partisan retaliation, 
preserving judicial independence, protecting classified information, and providing due process 
can intentionally slow immediate political consequence.

22. Discussion
American governmental development repeatedly follows a recognizable cycle:
Problem -> Authority -> Solution -> New Incentives -> New Vulnerability -> 
Failure -> Detection -> Reform -> New Authority
The missing variable in many accounts is consequence. A system should not be judged merely 
by whether it can recognize failure but by whether recognition changes future behavior.
The Confederation crisis produced institutional replacement. The Alien and Sedition 
controversy contributed to electoral transfer. Patronage produced civil-service reform. Teapot 
Dome produced imprisonment and stronger investigative precedent. Watergate produced 
presidential resignation and institutional reform. These are high-consequence events.
Modern government also provides cases in which sophisticated oversight systems repeatedly 
document persistent problems across many years. The contrast between high-detection systems 
and durable correction deserves direct measurement.
23. Conclusion
The evidence supports neither a romanticized view of original American government nor a 
simplistic theory of uninterrupted institutional decline. The founding generation's first national 
governmental framework proved inadequate in important respects, and the Constitution 
deliberately increased governmental power because effective government required greater 
capability.
American history subsequently demonstrates repeated episodes in which increased authority 
solved genuine problems and repeated episodes in which governmental authority created new 
opportunities for abuse, political influence, secrecy, administrative distance, or institutional 
failure.
American accountability mechanisms responded - sometimes remarkably well. But every 
corrective institution becomes part of the system. Every new layer can increase capability and 
can also increase Citizen Distance.
Authority must remain aligned with Responsibility. Responsibility must 
remain identifiable. Detection must remain connected to Consequence. 
Consequence must produce Correction. Correction must be independently 
verified.

THE 250-YEAR ACCOUNTABILITY DRIFT | 13
The American government of 2026 possesses accountability mechanisms the government of 
1789 could scarcely have imagined. It also possesses governmental capabilities and institutional 
complexity the founders could scarcely have imagined. The existence of more oversight 
therefore cannot by itself answer whether accountability improved.
Did accountability grow as fast as power?
The historical evidence suggests that the answer differs across periods and institutions. It shows
episodes of powerful accountability correction and identifies recurring conditions under which 
authority can become separated from consequence.
A government does not become accountable merely because it can document its own failures. 
Reports are not consequences. Hearings are not corrections. Investigations are not reforms. 
Transparency is not itself accountability. These mechanisms matter because they create the 
possibility of accountability. The final test comes afterward: Was responsibility identified? Did 
meaningful correction occur? Was the correction verified? Did the failure return?
The American constitutional experiment began by asking how power could be made 
accountable to the governed. Two hundred and fifty years later, the fundamental question has 
not changed. Only the scale of the system has.

Author's Note on Terminology
Accountability Drift, Consequence Failure, Citizen Distance, and the Detection-Correction Gap 
are analytical constructs proposed by this paper. They are not presented as established 
constitutional doctrines or standardized governmental metrics.
The framework is intended to be falsifiable. Evidence demonstrating that responsibility clarity, 
corrective consequence, independent oversight, and citizen control kept pace with or exceeded 
governmental expansion would weigh against the Accountability Drift hypothesis.
